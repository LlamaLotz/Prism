CHAPTER ONE: THE ATMOSPHERE

The atmosphere is a thin layer of gas held by gravity. Nitrogen and oxygen make up most of its mass. Trace gases absorb and emit radiation.

Pressure falls with altitude because fewer gas molecules sit above. Temperature changes in distinct layers. The troposphere contains most weather and water vapor.

The stratosphere contains ozone, which absorbs ultraviolet light. Ozone depletion increases surface ultraviolet exposure. International agreements phased out many ozone-depleting chemicals.

CHAPTER TWO: WEATHER SYSTEMS

Air masses form over land and ocean. Fronts mark boundaries between air masses. Warm fronts bring gradual cloudiness, while cold fronts can produce rapid storms.

Low-pressure systems draw air inward and upward. Rising air cools and condenses water vapor. Latent heat release strengthens storms.

Forecasting combines observations with numerical models. Models divide the atmosphere into a grid. Small errors grow over time, limiting forecast range.

CHAPTER THREE: CLIMATE

Climate describes long-term patterns rather than individual weather events. Greenhouse gases trap outgoing infrared radiation. Increasing concentrations warm the surface and lower atmosphere.

The ocean stores and transports heat. Currents redistribute warmth from the tropics toward the poles. Sea ice reflects sunlight and influences circulation.

Climate projections depend on emissions and on feedbacks. Clouds, water vapor, and ice each affect the response. Uncertainty is largest for regional precipitation.
