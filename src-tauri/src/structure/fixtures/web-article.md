Coral reefs are among the most productive ecosystems in the ocean. They cover less than one percent of the seafloor but support roughly a quarter of marine species. Reef-building corals are animals that host photosynthetic algae.

The partnership between coral and algae supplies most of the coral's energy. Warm water stresses the relationship and causes corals to expel their algae. This bleaching response leaves the coral pale and deprived of energy.

Reefs protect coastlines from waves and storms. They also support fisheries and tourism across tropical regions. The economic value of a healthy reef can exceed millions of dollars per year.

Restoration projects transplant coral fragments onto damaged reefs. Nurseries grow fragments until they are large enough for outplanting. Some projects select heat-tolerant genotypes to improve survival.

Local threats compound global warming. Overfishing removes grazers that control algae. Runoff carries sediment and nutrients that smother corals. Anchors and careless divers break fragile branches.

Monitoring tracks coral cover, bleaching severity, and fish abundance. Repeated surveys show whether restoration is working. Open data helps managers compare sites and adjust methods.
